# The Road Without a Crown

_A branching Crownless storybook_

## The milestone with four faces

It is never a good sign when the breakfast bell rings and no birds fly from
the bakery roof.

Bracken heard it first. The old horse lifted one ear and stopped in the road.
Morrow stopped beside him, because Morrow believed Bracken knew everything.
This was not true, but it was useful.

Ahead stood Thornford. Its chimneys smoked. Its river flashed. Its hill
granaries were round and fat as sleeping beetles.

Still, no birds flew from the bakery.

You climbed down from the carriage beside a mossy milestone. Three little
crowns had been cut into it. One was square, one was sharp, and one was so tall
that whoever carved it must have thought very highly of kings.

Beneath them, almost rubbed away, was a fourth mark.

Not a crown.

A wheel.

The same wheel was stamped on the brass charter box under your seat.

When you brushed the moss aside, something clicked inside the stone.

But then the breakfast bell rang again.

This time, a child shouted.

You left the stone unopened.

For now.

## Act I — The bell with no breakfast

### The girl in the bread line

The shouting child was a girl in one red mitten. Her other hand was bare and
closed around three grains of wheat.

"That is not enough for a loaf," you said.

"It is not even enough for a mouse's sandwich," she said. "I counted."

Her name was Nell Varo. She was standing at the front of a line that went past
the bakery, around the well, under a washing line, and very nearly back to the
bakery again.

The baker opened his door. Flour dusted his hair, but not his apron.

"No bread," he called.

The line made the saddest sound a crowd can make: almost no sound at all.

Nell opened her fingers. Two of her grains were pale. The third was black at
one end.

"They fell from a king's wagon," she whispered. "A full wagon. It went east
before sunrise. Empty wagons came back."

Across the square, soldiers shut the granary gate. Behind the bars, sacks rose
in neat yellow walls.

There was food in Thornford.

There was simply no breakfast.

Nell tied a red thread around your wrist.

"So you remember to wonder," she said.

### Mara's neat little offer

Mara Venn kept the granary keys on a ring at her belt. She had a narrow face,
a tidy coat, and the worried look of someone trying to hold six doors shut
with five keys.

She led you into her office. On the wall hung a road chart with a little iron
bridge painted in the east. Someone had crossed it out in black ink.

"Silverwick has sent three requests for flour," Mara said. "I can spare eight
sacks. One carriage load. Yours, if you take my seal and deliver every sack to
the guild store."

She put a white wax letter on the table.

"Why is the bridge closed?" you asked.

Mara straightened the letter until it was exactly square with the table.

"Because Alderwatch closed it."

This was an answer in the way an empty bowl is a meal.

You looked at the ink pot. A crust of black wax clung to its rim. The same
black wax marked Nell's burnt grain.

Mara noticed you noticing.

"Eighteen crowns on delivery," she said quickly. "And the thanks of very
important people."

"What about the thanks of the hungry people?"

Mara looked toward the granary yard.

"Those are more expensive."

### Tomas and the door that was not there

On your way out, a stack of flour barrels coughed.

Then it said, "Very important people rarely eat soup with the rest of us."

A man unfolded himself from behind the barrels. His name was Tomas Rill. He
wore a green scarf, polished boots, and the smile of a person who always knew
which window had been left unlocked.

"There is another road to Silverwick," Tomas said.

"It is not on Mara's chart."

"Then Mara's chart is behaving very well."

He rolled one barrel aside. Behind it was a door painted to look like a wall.
On the door someone had scratched a fox with a lantern in its mouth.

"Bring the flour through Gloamgate," he said. "Ask for the foxfire supper.
No soldiers. No inspection. Better pay."

"And what do you get?"

For one moment, Tomas stopped smiling.

"My sister works the lower mine. She wrote that they have boiled their seed
grain."

Then his smile returned, bright as a coin at the bottom of a muddy puddle.

"Also, better pay."

### The thing beneath the wagon

In the carriage yard, you found Jory Fen lying on his back beneath a broken
ore wagon.

Only his boots showed.

"If you are dead," you said, "your boots are being very calm about it."

"I am thinking," Jory replied.

He slid out. He was a young miner with silver dust in his eyebrows. Around his
neck hung a bent brass whistle.

"The bridge is not the only way," he said. "The old freight tunnel reaches
Silverwick. Or it did before the mine began breathing."

"Mines do not breathe."

The wagon rose and fell behind him.

In.

Out.

From beneath its wheels came a cold breath that smelled of wet stone and
something sweet.

Jory gave you the whistle.

"If you hear singing underground, don't answer with your own name."

"What should I answer with?"

"I was hoping you would know."

### What fits in a carriage

Eight sacks of Food filled one cargo bay.

Two bundles of Tools would fill another. They could mend a bridge winch, brace
a mine roof, or repair an axle after a fight.

There was room for guards, if you left something behind.

There was room for a traveller's note, if you could find one worth trusting.

There was one safe place for a sealed charter. Mara's white letter fit there
perfectly.

So did Tomas's quiet commission.

They did not fit together.

Bracken stamped one hoof. Morrow copied him.

The road was waiting.

## The first fork — three ways through Gloamgate

Gloamgate was a town built around a question.

Every street entered its round market. Every street left by another gate. The
people there sold maps, but no two maps agreed.

At the centre of the market stood an old fountain. Its stone queen had no
head. Someone had placed a wooden bowl where her crown should have been.

Nell's red thread pulled loose from your wrist and caught on the fountain.
Under it, scratched into the stone, was the wheel mark from the milestone.

A map seller saw you looking.

"Old carriage nonsense," she said. "Means the road belongs to whoever keeps
it open."

"Is that true?"

"It is what my grandmother told tax collectors."

She offered three notes.

One smelled of lamp oil and described the King's Road through Alderwatch.

One had been folded into the shape of a fox and described no road at all.

One was written on the back of a mine-safety prayer. Half the words had washed
away.

The carriage could follow only one before the flour spoiled.

Beside the notes lay a little brass bell shaped like a fox. The map seller
said Tomas had left it there and had been pretending not to look for it all
morning.

If you returned it, Tomas turned it over in his hand.

"Keep it," he said. "It does not ring for roads everyone can see."

## The King's Road

### Captain Ilyra's chain

The bridge at Alderwatch was not broken.

It stretched over the ravine in one proud iron line. Its boards were sound.
Its ropes were new. Its winch shone with fresh grease.

A chain held the gate shut.

Captain Ilyra Senn stood before it in a red cloak. She was not much taller
than you, but the gate seemed to take orders from her.

"Closed," she said.

"I can see that."

"Then the sign is working."

Behind her, a soldier carried a bowl to a boy sitting on the wall. The boy ate
too fast. Ilyra watched until he slowed down.

She had hungry people too.

"Silverwick needs this flour," you said.

"Alderwatch needs to know what crosses its bridge."

She held out one gloved hand.

You could put Mara's charter into it. The old wheel seal would make Ilyra go
quiet, though she would search every sack.

You could put two Tools into the hands of the bridge keeper. He would mend the
winch so loudly that everyone would hear there was never anything wrong with
it.

You could show Ilyra Nell's blackened grain. A matching smear marked the axle
of a military wagon waiting on the far side.

Or you could put your hand on your sword.

That was also an answer. It was simply the sort that made fewer answers
possible later.

Whichever truth you chose, Ilyra looked at you as if you had opened a door she
had been leaning against.

"My orders say no grain goes east," she said softly.

"Then why is there grain on that wagon?"

She looked over her shoulder.

The hungry boy on the wall had stopped eating.

That was when Ilyra broke her own chain.

Not for you.

For him.

Before you left, she returned Nell's black grain wrapped in a scrap of red
cloth.

"If anyone asks," she said, "the bridge defeated me in honourable combat."

You promised to describe the battle as extremely fierce.

## The Night Road

### Supper under the fox lantern

The fox-shaped note was not a map.

It was a paper lantern.

When you placed a candle inside it, tiny holes pricked a path of stars across
the table. Tomas turned the lantern until one star rested on Gloamgate's north
gate.

"Maps tell soldiers where to wait," he said. "Stars are more polite."

The Night Road began as a ditch, became a sheep track, disappeared beneath a
fallen tree, and returned when it was sure no one official was following.

At dusk, people in patched coats stepped out around the carriage. Their
captain wore a wooden fox mask.

"We take one thing from every wagon," the captain said.

"Coin?"

"Sometimes."

"Food?"

"Often."

"Names?"

The fox mask tilted.

"Only from people who deserve remembering."

A pot simmered over the campfire. It contained one onion, a bootlace, and a
great deal of hope.

You could pay the road price in crowns.

You could open one flour sack and make the pot into supper.

You could offer Tools to mend the camp's broken pump.

You could ask why the captain's left glove was tied with the same green cloth
as Tomas's scarf.

Or you could fight, if you wished to learn how many hungry people it takes to
block a narrow road.

When the mask finally came off, Tomas's sister stood beneath it.

"He said you were in the mine," you said.

"I was," she answered. "Then the guild locked the lower gate with twelve of
us outside it. Tomas tells kinder lies when he is frightened."

She ate one spoonful of supper and passed the bowl on.

"Take the road," she said. "But when you reach Silverwick, do not give all
eight sacks to the people who locked that gate."

The camp opened around the carriage.

As you left, Tomas ran beside Morrow and adjusted the little fox bell under
the driver's seat.

"Now it knows this road too," he said.

## The Road Under the Hill

### The mine that sang your name

The old freight tunnel began behind a silverworks in Gloamgate and sloped
east beneath the hills.

Jory walked ahead with a lamp. The flame leaned toward Silverwick, although
there was no wind.

"The mine is breathing out," he whispered.

"What happens when it breathes in?"

The flame went dark.

Some questions answer themselves.

In the darkness, something clicked across the rails. Then another thing
clicked. Then a hundred little feet found the same rhythm.

Jory's bent whistle began to hum.

From the flooded tunnel came a thin, sweet song.

It sang your name.

You remembered Jory's warning and did not answer.

The song tried Nell's name. Then Mara's. Then Bracken's.

Bracken, being sensible, did not answer either. Morrow looked tempted.

When Jory relit the lamp, pale stone-backed creatures ringed the water. They
had arranged lost spoons, buttons, nails, and mine tokens in careful spirals.
The miners called them thieves.

The creatures had put every object beside the place where it had fallen.

Beyond them, the old freight bridge sagged over their pool.

Two Tools could brace the bridge without touching the water.

A little Food placed in the dry shaft would draw the youngest creatures away
while the carriage crossed.

The fox bell, if you had found it before choosing this road, would make the
hidden stepping stones shine.

Steel would scatter the colony. It would also leave the water unguarded when
the deeper things woke.

Or you could blow Jory's whistle without giving it a name.

You blew three notes: one for Bracken, one for Morrow, and one for the road.

The largest creature lifted its head.

Then it sang the same three notes back.

Jory laughed so suddenly that the mine echoed with it.

"It was never calling us," he said. "It was asking who belonged to the
carriage."

The creatures moved their silver spirals aside. Beneath them was the old
wheel mark, set into the floor long before the mine had a name.

## The road you did not take

There were other things to carry.

Silk paid better than flour. Gems weighed less. A merchant in Gloamgate would
buy every cargo space you had and ask no questions until the coins were
counted.

If you chose that work, the game did not end.

At the next inn, your silk tablecloth was clean and your supper bowl was full.
Across the room, a Silverwick family shared one potato.

Nell's red thread was still around your wrist.

You could untie it.

It would leave a mark.

## The ovens of Silverwick

Silverwick climbed the hill in black stone steps. Its chimneys coughed silver
dust. Its market clock had stopped at breakfast.

At the public ovens, children waited with empty tins.

At the guild store, a clerk waited with Mara's receipt.

In a side alley, a woman in a fox mask waited with a cart.

Jory's mother waited nowhere. She was working below ground because hungry
people do not always have time to stand where heroes can find them.

The eight sacks belonged to your carriage until you opened the doors.

You could honour Mara's letter and unload at the guild.

You could sell to the quiet market and let Tomas's sister divide the flour.

You could carry the sacks past both of them and pour the first one straight
into the public oven hopper.

You could keep them closed.

No golden light told you which choice was good.

Instead, people watched.

The guild clerk watched the receipt.

The fox captain watched the children.

Jory watched you.

So did a little girl with one red mitten who had somehow arrived before you.

"I took the short road," Nell said.

"There was a short road?"

"There is when you are not pulling a carriage."

After the flour was given—or not given—Nell climbed onto an oven bench. She
found an old rhyme scratched in the wood.

> Wheat for the road and iron for the door,
>
> Roses for the promise that was broken before.
>
> Three bright things where the old smoke curls,
>
> And no true crown in the kingdoms of the world.

"What does it mean?" Nell asked.

No one answered.

But the guild clerk quietly removed his hat.

### A letter one month later

Thirty days passed.

One morning, a letter waited on the driver's seat.

If you opened the bridge, Ilyra had written it. She did not say thank you. She
included the broken chain link and wrote, _The boy on the wall now eats before
the officers do._

If you took the Night Road, Tomas had written it. The page smelled of onion
soup. He drew a fox beside a new public toll sign and wrote, _My sister hates
the sign. She painted it herself._

If you crossed the mine, Jory had written it. Silver dust glittered in the
fold. He wrote, _The stonebacks returned three lost wedding rings. The guild
still calls them thieves._

If you kept the flour, the letter came from Nell. It held only the red thread.

None of the letters explained the rhyme.

All of them ended with the same line:

_Come to Gloamgate. Two royal riders are asking for the Crownless carriage._

## Act II: Two riders in the snow

### White wax, red wax

Snow was falling when you returned to Gloamgate.

Two horses had collapsed beside the headless fountain. Their riders were still
arguing.

Brin Alder wore a white scarf and carried a letter sealed in white wax. Cera
Mott wore a red scarf and carried a letter sealed in red wax.

"Mine must reach Rosespire first," said Brin.

"Mine must reach Alderwatch first," said Cera.

"Mine can stop a war."

"Mine can stop a dragon."

"One of you is lying," you said.

"Only one?" Tomas asked from the fountain.

The charter box under your seat had room for one royal letter.

Before you chose, you had time to learn something about each rider.

Brin spent his last coin on warm mash for both fallen horses. When he thought
no one was looking, he pressed the white letter to his forehead as if it were
a prayer.

Cera mended a wooden bird for a child in the square. Her fingers shook each
time the palace bell rang. When the child asked if she was afraid, Cera said,
"Constantly. It saves time."

If you asked Brin about the war, he showed you a little drawing made by his
brother in Alderwatch. In the corner stood the hungry boy from the bridge.

If you asked Cera about the dragon, she showed you a scale scorched white at
the edge. She had found it beside an empty royal wagon.

The wheel mark from your charter was pressed faintly beneath both seals.

Neither rider knew why.

You could carry the white letter.

You could carry the red letter.

You could place both on the fountain and break both seals where the market
could see.

You could leave them to their own tired horses.

The choice did not decide which rider was good.

It decided which secret got there first.

### The road remembers you

The road had not forgotten the first act.

At Alderwatch, Ilyra opened the bridge before you asked—or closed it herself
if your earlier answer had cost her people.

On the Night Road, Tomas's sister hid the chosen letter—or copied it and kept
the copy where hungry people might read it.

Under the hill, the stonebacks led you around a fresh cave-in—or watched from
the water if steel had taught them not to trust carriage bells.

If you had carried silk while Silverwick starved, every road took longer.
Hungry travellers had built little fires at the junctions. Some asked for
Food. Some asked for coin. Some asked why your tablecloths were so clean.

The first choice had not become a number.

It had become the road.

### The room behind the throne

Rosespire palace had a throne tall enough for a king and a door small enough
for a servant.

Nell found the small door.

"I took the short road again," she said.

Behind the throne was a quiet room lined with blue velvet. Three shapes were
clean where the dust had not settled:

A round shape, like a ring of wheat.

A long shape, like an iron key.

A curved shape, like a crown made from rose thorns.

All three were gone.

On the floor lay a charcoal rubbing from a cave wall. It showed a sleeping
dragon curled around those same three shapes.

In one corner, someone had drawn the old wheel.

The king entered through the tall door. Without his court around him, he
looked smaller than his throne.

If you brought the white letter, he read it twice. Then he asked everyone to
leave except the person who carried it.

If you brought the red letter, he rang the muster bell. Far below, armourers
lit their fires.

If you opened both letters in the market, the king already knew. So did the
bakers, soldiers, miners, outlaws, and children pressed against his gates.

If you brought neither, a snow-wet crow struck the window. A torn strip of red
wax hung from its leg. No one could read the words that remained.

"Where are the three things?" Nell asked.

The king looked at the clean shapes on the velvet.

"Safe," he said.

From somewhere beyond the palace roof came a sound like a mountain turning in
its sleep.

Ash fell upward past the window.

The king did not look up.

"Very safe," he said.

## Act III — The court beneath the cave

### Nara Soot-Tongue remembers

The road to the dragon cave passed Hollowbarrow and ended at a black hill.

Goblins lived beneath it.

They did not leap from shadows or wave rusty swords. They sat at long desks,
copying names into books.

One wrote _silver spoon, gift from a bride_ beside a picture of a spoon.

Another wrote _red marble, lost by a child who lied about losing it_.

At the last desk sat Nara Soot-Tongue. She wore seven necklaces and spectacles
with only one lens.

"At last," she said when she saw your charter. "The wheel has come back for
its road."

"What wheel?"

Nara shut her book.

"An excellent question. We have waited three hundred years for someone to ask
it after bringing lunch."

She looked into the carriage.

If you had traded fairly on the Night Road, Nara already knew your name. News
travels quickly between people whom kings call thieves.

If you had spared the stonebacks, a line of silver buttons on her desk had
been returned by the colony.

If you had opened the bridge, she had a copy of Ilyra's broken order. Goblins
kept excellent records of broken promises.

If you had done none of those things, Nara remained polite. Politeness, she
explained, was especially useful when one had hidden archers.

You could trade Food for passage.

You could escort the next tribute cart and enter beside it.

You could follow a smuggled cave note.

You could threaten the goblins and make the cave easier to enter today.

Or you could sit at Nara's table and listen.

Listening took time.

It also revealed that each treasure in the hoard still had a name, an owner,
and a story.

The dragon was not sleeping on gold.

It was sleeping on memories.

### Three bright things

The dragon cave was warmer than a kitchen and quieter than a church.

At its centre slept the oldest creature you had ever seen.

Its scales were the colour of banked coals. One wing covered most of the
hoard. The other twitched whenever Nara turned a page in her book far below.

Beneath one claw lay the Wheat Ring. Tiny carved mice chased one another
around its rim. Thornford's first farmers had given it to the old empire in
exchange for a road that did not flood.

Under the dragon's chin lay the Iron Key. It had never opened a door. It had
opened a promise: Alderwatch would guard the bridge, but never own it.

Beside one sleeping eye rested the Rose Circlet. One thorn was missing. The
missing thorn was stamped into the wax of every royal letter in Rosespire.

The old rhyme clicked together like three pieces of a toy.

Not an answer.

A choice.

The carriage could carry all three, but then it could carry almost nothing
else.

Taking even one would wake the debt, if not the dragon. Wells would steam.
Birds would leave. Ash would rise. Someone would have to return the object or
pay its true value.

You could take one piece and leave room for repayment.

You could take all three and trust a royal promise.

You could copy their names and leave them in the hoard.

You could wait for Cera's army and decide whom to help when it arrived.

You could wake the dragon and ask why it kept the crowns.

If you asked, one ember-bright eye opened.

"Kept?" the dragon murmured.

The word rolled stones from the ceiling.

"Little wheel, I kept nothing. They brought these to me when their promises
became too heavy to carry."

The dragon closed its eye.

"Humans always return for the shiny part of a promise."

## The last fork

Below the cave, three roads waited.

One climbed to Rosespire, where the king had cleared a place above his throne.

One bent toward Gloamgate, where the headless fountain still wore its wooden
bowl.

One wound back to Nara's court and the sleeping dragon.

The old charter lay open on the driver's seat.

In moonlight, writing appeared beneath the wheel seal.

You expected the name of an emperor.

You expected a royal command.

You expected, at the very least, a useful map.

The faded words said only:

_Let the road belong to those who keep it open._

Bracken looked down the first road.

Morrow looked down the second.

Nell looked back at the third.

"Well?" she asked.

The carriage could not take every road.

That was what made it yours.

## The Shared Table

The king received the three treasures in the public square, because you would
not carry them through the small servant's door.

Mara read the account aloud.

Ilyra brought the broken bridge chain.

Tomas's sister brought the Night Road toll book.

Jory brought a silver spiral made by the stonebacks.

Nara brought the dragon's bill.

It was a very long bill.

The king sold one of his own towers to pay it. No one missed the tower. It had
mostly been used for looking down at people.

That winter, the Wheat Ring hung above Silverwick's public ovens. The Iron Key
hung at the open bridge. The Rose Circlet remained in Rosespire, but only after
the king signed his promises beneath the old wheel mark.

At the first spring feast, every important person was given a small bowl.

Every hungry child was given a large one.

Nell said this was the first sensible law any crown had made.

## The Iron Crown

Cera's muster reached the cave before the first thaw.

The dragon rose. The kingdoms stood together for one bright, terrible hour.

When it ended, soldiers carried the Wheat Ring, Iron Key, and Rose Circlet to
Rosespire behind twelve red banners.

The king wore the circlet. Alderwatch guarded every bridge. Grain moved on
time. Bandits vanished from the main road.

So did musicians without permits, children without shoes, goblins without
papers, and fox lanterns after dark.

The markets were full.

The gates were fuller.

Years later, travellers still argued about whether the realm had become safe
or merely shut.

Ilyra never argued.

She kept one broken chain link in her pocket.

## The Road Without Kings

You took the treasures to Gloamgate.

The map seller placed them in the archive, each beside its true account. The
king sent guards. The Night Road sent fox masks. Silverwick sent miners.
Thornford sent bakers with very hard rolling pins.

No one could agree who should own the treasures.

So Nell put the wooden bowl from the fountain between them.

"If you want a crown," she said, "first fill this."

The argument lasted all winter.

The bowl stayed full.

By spring, the towns had formed a carriage league. Its first rule was that no
one could close a road without feeding the people at both ends.

Its second rule was that Tomas could not write the rules.

He wrote them anyway, but Nell checked his spelling.

## The Crown of Ash

The king put on the Rose Circlet before the dragon's debt was paid.

For seven days, the wells steamed.

For seven nights, no birds crossed the moon.

On the fourteenth morning, ash rose from the palace chimneys before any fire
was lit.

The dragon came at sunset.

When it left, the throne room had no roof. The king still wore his bright
crown beneath an open sky.

People could see it from every ruined street.

They could also see him.

That winter, no one called the crown lost.

They called it expensive.

## The Empty Seat

You left the three treasures beneath the dragon's wing.

Perhaps you carried silk instead of flour. Perhaps neither letter reached its
court. Perhaps you simply decided that three kingdoms had made their own mess
and owned good brooms.

The world did not wait.

Silverwick ate its seed grain. Alderwatch held its bridge. The Night Road grew
into a town with no walls. One royal courier arrived late. The other never
arrived.

In Rosespire, dust covered the clean shapes behind the throne until no one
remembered what had hung there.

But children remembered the carriage.

They drew the old wheel on milestones and bakery doors. Sometimes it meant a
safe road. Sometimes it meant food was hidden nearby. Sometimes it meant only:

_Someone came this way and had a choice._

Years later, when Bracken and Morrow were old enough to disagree with the
weather, you returned to Thornford.

Birds flew from the bakery roof.

Nell, now the baker, handed you a small loaf.

"Enough for a mouse's sandwich?" you asked.

"Two," she said.

Then the breakfast bell rang.

This time, the town smelled of bread.

## Story structure for implementation

The release version ends after the Silverwick ovens and the first delayed
letter. The Winter Courier and Hollow Crown acts extend the same relationships
later.

Each route has three character beats:

1. A public face: Ilyra the hard captain, Tomas the charming liar, or Jory the
   nervous miner.
2. A private contradiction discovered by watching or helping them.
3. A later message or changed action that pays off what the player learned.

The old wheel, black wax, empty throne shapes, and children's rhyme reveal the
larger mystery in pieces. No character gives the player a full history speech.
The player can understand enough to act before knowing the whole truth.

The main facts remain simulation-backed: food is carried, roads remember what
happened, letters travel, treasures have owners, and dragon theft creates a
debt. The prose reveals those facts through people, objects, and changed places
after the simulation commits them.
