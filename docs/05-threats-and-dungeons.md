# Bandits, Goblins, Dragons, Monsters, and Dungeons

## Three distinct threat families

Threats should not collapse into interchangeable combat encounters.

- **Bandits** are political and economic actors.
- **Goblins** are a cult, a raiding economy, and a moving tribute chain.
- **The dragon** is a sleeping power that retaliates against theft, not wealth.
- **Monsters** belong to a lightweight ecology.
- **Dungeons** are persistent historical wounds or opportunities.

Each family connects differently to settlements, routes, factions, and player
choices.

## Bandit factions

Bandits may originate from:

- Deserters after war
- Displaced farmers or workers
- Rebels defined as criminals by a ruler
- Organized criminal families
- Smugglers pushed toward violence
- Unpaid mercenary companies
- Hunters displaced by monster pressure

A bandit group tracks:

- Membership and recruitment pressure
- Leader and internal alignment
- Camp or controlled route
- Food, weapons, and money
- Relationships with settlements and factions
- Preferred targets
- Tolerance for negotiation
- Current strategic goal

Bandits raid shipments because they need cargo, seek leverage, serve a patron,
or want control—not because an encounter meter filled.

### No-man's-land camps and expeditions

Bandit groups live on contested cross-border roads. Their home grows from a
hideout into a camp, war camp, and eventually an outlaw town. Each tier has its
own small service budget, starting with things such as a black market, stable, smithy,
or healer rather than copying a lawful settlement.

A raid is persistent travel, not an instant dice roll:

1. The camp scores the real settlements at the ends of its road.
2. Scouts choose a target and the good with the best available stock.
3. The group musters and travels for the route's real travel time.
4. On arrival it removes goods from the target's actual stock and damages local
   security and prosperity.
5. The expedition travels home with the loot.
6. Returned supplies support recruitment, influence, camp growth, and later
   services.

Meeting and weakening that bandit group on the same road changes the force able
to complete the expedition.

### Bandit resolutions

The player may:

- Destroy or disperse the group
- Kill, capture, replace, or bargain with its leader
- Pay or negotiate passage
- Redirect attacks toward a rival
- Expose an official patron
- Address recruitment causes
- Help the group become a militia, settlement, or recognized faction

Destroying a camp without changing recruitment pressure may create a successor
group later.

## Goblin tribute and the dragon hoard

Prosperity does not make the dragon attack. Goblins are a material faction with
a lair, food, equipment, cargo, and travel time. The full chain is physical and
recorded:

1. A hungry lair raids Food. A poorly equipped lair raids Iron, Tools, or
   Weapons. A stable lair seeks portable offerings.
2. A goblin band leaves its lair and travels toward a real farm, market, or
   caravan target.
3. It removes goods, market coin, or a named treasure from that target and
   carries the loot back to the lair.
4. Food and equipment enter the lair stores. Raw Gold, Gems, coin, and named
   treasures are prepared as offerings.
5. A tribute carrier makes a second physical journey from the lair to the
   dragon cave.
6. Only delivery transfers ownership to the dragon. Intercepting the carrier
   before delivery does not anger it.
7. The dragon remains calm unless someone later steals from the delivered
   hoard.

### Why anyone robs a dragon

Social inequality creates the hoard thieves. The simulation compares hunger
with visible prosperity, food prices, coin held in the local market and kingdom
treasury, and the political strength of the commoners. Wealth alone is not enough. When hungry families
live beside guarded wealth and have little lawful power, the Ash-Poor Company
may form.

The company travels from the unequal settlement to the cave. It steals a real
amount from the hoard, returns home, and spends the money on bread and old
debts. The relief is real but brief. Omens gather around the thieves' home. If
the debt remains unpaid, the dragon burns it. After the first burning, the
wealthy government repays the dragon from its treasury. The rulers finally
spend the guarded money, but only after commoners and homes have paid the cost.

War creates a second motive. Unsafe and restricted frontier roads raise local war
burden. The crown must move treasury gold into a local war chest. That chest
pays wages into the town market and buys wheat, tools, and weapons from named suppliers;
the goods then travel in ordinary shipments. If a heavily exposed fortress or
capital lacks supplies and liquid war gold while legitimacy is weak, its court
may send the Crown Levy. The levy travels to the same cave, steals from the same
hoard, and carries the money back into the real war chest. War itself never
angers the dragon. The human decision to steal does.

The player may also enter the cave and steal a chosen amount. Either theft creates 14 days
of omens around a named settlement: smoke sinks into chimneys, wells grow hot,
and animals flee. Returning all outstanding treasure ends the threat. Partial
repayment does not reset the omen clock. If the debt
remains, the dragon burns the named town, its stores, one service, nearby roads,
population, security, and prosperity. It then names another target and gives a
new 28-day course of omens. This repeats until the debt is repaid.

This is an OSR bargain, not a level-scaled encounter. The cave offers real
treasure before the player is ready to absorb the cost. The important question
is not whether the player can beat a dragon in fair combat. It is whether the
player understands whose homes secure the stolen wealth and what returning it
would cost.

### Dragon constraints

- No town burns merely for being rich.
- Every attack has a recorded hoard theft and omen as its parents.
- Goblin tribute and dragon retaliation are separate systems.
- Inequality causes a physical theft expedition; it never angers the dragon
  directly.
- War can cause a physical Crown Levy expedition; it never angers the dragon
  directly.
- Goblin loot leaves real market stores, lair stores, or treasure ownership,
  travels twice, and enters the hoard only on delivery.
- War never deletes a weekly fee: each crown moves through treasury, war chest,
  market, supplier, levy, or dragon hoard ledgers.
- Garrison wheat, tools, and weapons come from settlement stock and physical shipments.
- The exact stolen debt survives save, load, and replay.
- Repayment is always possible while the player still carries the money.
- Fire is severe but bounded; it damages a settlement instead of deleting it.
- Social thieves and the player use the same hoard, debt, omen, and fire rules.
- An automatic theft cannot create endless unattended fire: after the first
  attack, the responsible kingdom makes restitution from the war chest,
  treasury, and then existing household coin. Repayment never creates gold by
  converting destroyed food into money.
- Goblins defend the cave against automatic hoard thieves. Their defence spends
  real Weapons and members, which sends them back out to raid for replacement gear.
- A continuing social or war crisis cannot launch the same raid every year. Its
  pressure must ease before that motive can rise again.

## Debt, sanctions, and night roads

The Iron Ledger is a network of monasteries holding copied accounts and real
deposited crowns. It lends those crowns for famine food and productive Tools.
The borrowing kingdom records debt; treasury payments and market tithes return
the same crowns to the monastery reserve. Solvent kingdoms can deposit surplus
treasury coin, so wealth can circulate without creating money.

A route marked restricted is not deleted. Its condition halves freight
capacity, and an official war border halves it again. Ordinary border traffic
pays sanctions, a damaged road charges tolls, and a night road takes bribes.
These payments move into a real kingdom treasury.
High hunger or a realm near its credit limit can open more night roads. Debt and
hunger also recruit bandits, but an unfed bandit camp loses members and cannot
raid a town whose stores are already empty.

Goblin tribute raids can strip portable treasure and exposed coin. There is no
artificial cash floor and no invisible limit on the hoard. If goblins and the
dragon drain too much working coin, that is a real strategic crisis: kingdoms
can answer it by forming an alliance and risking a physical expedition against
the cave.

## Couriers, alliances, and slaying the dragon

Kings create diplomatic intentions, not instant facts. Declarations of war,
peace offers, calls for an anti-dragon alliance, and muster orders travel as
sealed courier messages. Couriers choose actual roads, take their travel time,
and face route danger. A damaged message may be lost; a weak or corrupt court
may suppress it or record the wrong meaning. The Crownless Carriage can accept
one of these messages as its current charter and physically deliver it.

When the dragon holds roughly a third of all tracked crowns, courts begin
sending alliance calls. Delivered muster orders pledge real kingdoms. Two or
more pledged realms may assemble a host only if their towns can supply 32 Food,
8 Tools, and 12 Weapons. These goods leave the towns and travel with the host.
The cave battle measures this supplied force against the dragon and its goblin
worshippers. Defeat destroys the expedition's supplies and hurts its mustering
town. Victory kills the dragon, breaks goblin devotion, and puts the dragon's
actual crowns, cave goods, and named treasures into a returning train. Nothing
is minted as a reward. The recovered crowns are divided between the allied
treasuries when the host gets home; remaining goods and treasures are unloaded
at its mustering town.

The dragon's death does not delete the goblins. Without their god they cease
tribute, but their lair still consumes Food and still needs Tools and Weapons.
Shortages send the surviving goblins back onto the roads as ordinary raiders.

## Smuggling and black markets

Smuggling exists because law and material demand disagree. Networks consist of:

- Suppliers
- Hidden routes
- Carriers and safehouses
- Corrupt or sympathetic officials
- Urban black markets
- Enforcement and protection relationships

Smuggling can relieve a destructive shortage, enrich violent groups, undermine
a blockade, move refugees, or spread dangerous relics. It is not a separate
shop with universally better prices.

## Monster ecology

Monster groups track only variables capable of producing play:

- Species or family
- Habitat
- Population pressure
- Food or magical energy source
- Nest or spawning site
- Territory and migration direction
- Competition or predation relationships
- Hunting pressure
- Regional effects

The model is deliberately lighter than the political economy. It exists to
explain where monsters appear, why they move, and how interventions change
future danger.

### Monster consequences

- Predators follow refugees or livestock.
- River creatures interrupt barge traffic.
- Burrowing colonies damage mines and roads.
- Corrupted wildlife spreads from an unsealed ruin.
- Removing one predator allows another population to expand.
- Monster materials support medicine, alchemy, or specialist equipment.
- Overhunting can destroy a settlement's dependent industry.

### Monster interventions

The player may hunt, trap, relocate, protect, exploit, contain, or investigate
monsters. Killing every visible creature should not resolve a habitat problem
unless the nest or causal pressure is addressed.

## Persistent dungeons

A dungeon begins with six answers:

1. What was it originally?
2. Why was it abandoned or sealed?
3. Who or what controls it now?
4. What does it release into the surrounding province?
5. Why do people continue to desire access?
6. Which long-term states can it enter?

Possible origins include:

- Mine
- Border fortress
- Tomb or ossuary
- Temple or pilgrimage complex
- Aqueduct or sewer
- Observatory or magical laboratory
- Buried town
- Monster-made colony

## Dungeon generation

Dungeons use authored spatial grammars parameterized by origin, damage,
occupants, ecology, and current strategic state. Generation should create:

- A legible route and optional branches
- Persistent shortcuts
- Areas belonging to different occupants
- Environmental evidence of history
- Resource and expedition pressure
- Decisions that do not require combat
- At least one connection to the surrounding province

Supply and carriage preparation determine expedition range. Dungeons should
support retreat, resupply, and return rather than assuming one uninterrupted
clear.

## Dungeon outcomes

A dungeon can become:

- Sealed or destroyed
- Reoccupied by monsters
- Controlled by a political faction
- Reopened as a mine or route
- Converted into a refuge
- Claimed as a fortress
- Established as a shrine or research site
- Used as a smuggling passage
- Developed into a carriage station

The resulting state changes production, migration, faction power, route
availability, monster pressure, and local presentation.

## Threat-generation constraints

- Every major bandit group has a human cause and material support.
- Every monster outbreak has a habitat, source, or displacement cause.
- Every dungeon has a persistent regional effect or opportunity.
- Every dragon retaliation has a specific unpaid theft as its cause.
- Threats cannot demand attention solely because enough time passed.
- Resolving symptoms without causes produces bounded, understandable recurrence.
- No region surfaces more than one major threat situation of the same family at
  a time.
