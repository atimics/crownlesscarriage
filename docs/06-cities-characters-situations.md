# Cities, Characters, and Situations

## City purpose

Cities are where simulation becomes emotionally legible. A city map is not a
complete simulation of every resident. It is a carefully constructed projection
of strategic state through recurring places, people, behaviours, and services.

## City grammars

A settlement's economic purpose shapes:

- Street topology and entrances
- Warehouses, workshops, fields, yards, or docks
- Worker and elite districts
- Civic and faction institutions
- Carriage infrastructure
- Black-market access
- Nearby hazards and dungeon connections
- Which buildings can change purpose

The first slice uses authored layout grammars with procedural arrangement and
state-dependent dressing. Fully procedural city topology remains deferred until
authored grammars prove distinct and readable.

## Strategic projection

Every important strategic variable must affect at least two visible channels.

| Strategic condition | Local projections |
| --- | --- |
| Food shortage | Empty stalls, queues, soup kitchens, guarded granaries, hunger dialogue |
| High prosperity | Construction, traffic, stocked shops, celebrations, new services |
| Unemployment | Idle workers, closed workshops, recruitment by factions and bandits |
| Weak security | Absent patrols, shuttered shops, protection demands, visible criminal control |
| Harsh enforcement | Checkpoints, searches, confiscation, prison traffic, hidden markets |
| Refugee pressure | Temporary camps, crowded inns, labour changes, political speeches |
| Monster pressure | Barricades, hunters, damaged livestock, specialist markets, curfews |
| Dungeon exploitation | Salvage yards, expedition traffic, quarantine, new wealth and corruption |

The projection system selects bounded combinations. It should not attempt to
display every statistic simultaneously.

## Interiors

Most interiors use reusable structural shells whose ownership, stock,
occupancy, services, and dressing reflect the simulation.

Priority interiors include:

- Coaching inn and company depot
- Market and warehouse
- Faction office or court
- Workshop tied to the settlement's function
- Black market or safehouse
- Clinic, shrine, or relief centre
- Residence belonging to a recurring character

Interiors may close, change owners, become occupied by another faction, or be
repurposed after a crisis.

## Named-character promotion

The simulation aggregates most residents. A person becomes a stable named
entity when they:

- Represent an institution or faction
- Anchor a playable situation
- Become a passenger or companion
- Hold responsibility for a persistent place
- Repeatedly interact with the player
- Are required to remember a bargain, betrayal, debt, or loss

Promotion creates identity, interests, relationships, location, role, and
memory. It does not require generating a complete biography or daily financial
history.

## Character interests

Important characters need:

- A material stake
- A desired outcome
- Something they can offer
- Something they fear losing
- A relationship to another interested actor
- A limited and believable instrument of power

Dialogue is authored from situation templates and verified ledger facts. The
game does not generate unrestricted prose that may contradict the simulation.

## From event to situation

The causal ledger may contain many events. A situation compiler turns a small
number into playable structures.

### Situation roles

- Affected group
- Initiating or responsible actor
- Beneficiary
- Opposing actor
- Witness or information source
- Optional hidden participant

One character may occupy several roles, but the conflict must remain legible.

### Situation phases

1. **Visible symptom:** the player witnesses a concrete change.
2. **Human anchors:** recurring people provide incompatible interpretations.
3. **Investigation:** the player discovers a concise causal chain.
4. **Commitment:** manifest, route, time, or allegiance choices close alternatives.
5. **Journey dilemma:** the road embodies the crisis.
6. **Local intervention:** combat, negotiation, exploration, delivery, or refusal.
7. **Immediate projection:** a place and character visibly change.
8. **Delayed echo:** a later event reveals broader or unintended effects.

## Situation invalidation

Autonomous worlds can change while the player travels. Every situation defines:

- Which state must remain true
- What happens if another actor resolves part of it
- Whether the contract transforms, closes, or offers a new choice
- How deposits, promises, and reputation are handled

The game should acknowledge changed circumstances rather than freezing the
world or pretending an obsolete objective remains valid.

## Information and rumours

The first slice delays knowledge through event packets rather than simulating
free-form gossip.

A packet contains:

- Originating event
- Sender or transmission channel
- Creation and arrival dates
- Accuracy and omitted facts
- Intended recipient

Basic stakes and system rules remain clear. Uncertainty focuses on motive,
responsibility, and what a faction intends to do.

## Character-scale play

### Exploration

Exploration reveals shortcuts, evidence, resources, habitats, political spaces,
and persistent location changes. Discoveries matter because they alter future
journeys or strategic options.

### Combat

Combat distinguishes:

- Human opponents who value survival, morale, position, and negotiation
- Monsters whose behaviour follows ecology and anatomy
- Dungeon guardians shaped by their original purpose

Victory produces explicit outcomes such as lost faction manpower, dispersed
bandits, reduced nest pressure, captured leaders, damaged infrastructure, or
recovered cargo.

### Non-combat resolution

Negotiation, bribery, concealment, legal authority, investigation, route choice,
and sacrifice must be first-class resolutions. Avoiding combat still consumes
resources, reputation, time, information, or opportunity.
